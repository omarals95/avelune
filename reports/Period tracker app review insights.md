# Paywalls and rigid predictions push users away

Across Flo and its competitors, users are not leaving because cycle tracking is hard to build. They leave because apps **charge for features that used to be free, apply average-cycle predictions to bodies that are not average, lose years of history, and treat health data carelessly**. Flo is the clearest case. In a primary sample of **1,250 App Store reviews from February to November 2026, 46% were 1–2★**, even though Flo's lifetime rating is 4.74★. About **65% of those low ratings cite price, Premium or subscriptions**, and about 30% cite upsell pop-ups ([App Store RSS](https://itunes.apple.com/us/rss/customerreviews/id=1038369065/sortby=mostrecent/json)). Clue, Glow/Eve, Premom, Stardust, Bellabeat and ad-supported calendar apps draw the same paywall complaints at smaller scale. The second complaint common to every category is prediction logic that ignores context: PCOS, endometriosis, IUDs, coming off the pill, postpartum, perimenopause and pregnancy loss. The third is data permanence: lost history on a new phone, wearable updates that erase or rewrite readings, and exports no other app can read. Privacy-first apps (Euki, drip, Periodical, Mensinator) solve the trust problem but lack fertile-window estimates, automatic backup, widgets and polish. Avelune already avoids the biggest complaint by having no paywall or ads, and its local-first, encrypted design avoids most of the privacy ones. The evidence says its next gains are **context-aware predictions, life-stage transitions, effortless encrypted backup, importers from Flo and Clue, and deeper condition, contraception and wearable tracking**. A caveat on evidence: only the Flo data is a large primary review sample. Notes on the other apps mostly rely on search-index snippets, store summaries, issue trackers and press, because Reddit, Trustpilot and most store pages could not be fetched. Theme counts for those apps are counts of sources, not counts of reviews.

## Flo's 1,250 recent reviews turn on money, not tracking

Flo's headline numbers look strong: **4.74★ from 2,012,676 iOS ratings** ([iTunes lookup](https://itunes.apple.com/lookup?id=1038369065&country=us)) and 4.7★ from about 5.1M Google Play reviews ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=org.iggymedia.periodtracker&hl=en_US&gl=US)). The recent review stream looks very different. In the 2026 sample from the US, GB, CA and AU storefronts, 419 reviews were 1★, 158 were 2★, 140 were 3★, 130 were 4★ and 403 were 5★. Themes were keyword-coded, so the counts below are approximate, but they are reliable enough to rank themes ([App Store RSS US](https://itunes.apple.com/us/rss/customerreviews/id=1038369065/sortby=mostrecent/json), [GB](https://itunes.apple.com/gb/rss/customerreviews/id=1038369065/sortby=mostrecent/json), [CA](https://itunes.apple.com/ca/rss/customerreviews/id=1038369065/sortby=mostrecent/json), [AU](https://itunes.apple.com/au/rss/customerreviews/id=1038369065/sortby=mostrecent/json)).

### Negative points: a hard paywall, dark patterns and missing context

The most charged 2026 grievance is **a hard paywall reached after a long, intimate onboarding questionnaire**. "Asks you a million questions then gives you a bill … after having me sign a disclaimer allowing them to use share and keep my data. And in the end says there's no free version. Feels like a scam," wrote one US reviewer in October 2026 ([App Store RSS US](https://itunes.apple.com/us/rss/customerreviews/id=1038369065/sortby=mostrecent/json)). Some reviewers still describe a free tier, so the hard paywall probably applies to new sign-ups or test cohorts rather than to everyone. Flo has not confirmed this. Long-time users describe **features moving behind Premium**: cycle-length history ("I can't even find how long my cycle was anymore without manually counting"), the ovulation day, notifications, insights, and pregnancy mode, which now carries an additional fee ([App Store RSS AU](https://itunes.apple.com/au/rss/customerreviews/id=1038369065/sortby=mostrecent/json)). The prices users quote range from "10 quid" a month in the UK to "$49.99 a month" in Australia. Upsells take several taps to dismiss ("tap 'unlock sale' and then press 'x' and THEN say no AGAIN"), use guilt-trip characters, and **still appear for paying Premium users**. Billing complaints centre on web subscriptions bought through Facebook quizzes, which cannot be cancelled in the App Store and leave users stuck in a loop between the app and the website. Users also report annual renewals with no reminder and refunds that are refused ([App Store RSS AU](https://itunes.apple.com/au/rss/customerreviews/id=1038369065/sortby=mostrecent/json); [CA](https://itunes.apple.com/ca/rss/customerreviews/id=1038369065/sortby=mostrecent/json)).

Prediction complaints are **less about the algorithm than about missing context fields**. Users who are postpartum or breastfeeding are told they may have PCOS or be in perimenopause, and "there is nowhere to get it to ignore that alert." Users with PCOS are assumed to be pregnant when a period is late. The app "forgets" an IUD, has no field for coming off the pill, ignores ovulation-test results, and after a logged miscarriage keeps showing daily "overdue" pop-ups ([App Store RSS AU](https://itunes.apple.com/au/rss/customerreviews/id=1038369065/sortby=mostrecent/json); [CA](https://itunes.apple.com/ca/rss/customerreviews/id=1038369065/sortby=mostrecent/json)). Reliability problems add to the list. A 2026 calendar bug shows the wrong weekday for dates and has lasted for months. Users report data loss ("Flo deleted all my data since 2018"), launch freezes and a widget that stopped working. Flo also **removed Apple Health sync without announcement**, which drew about 14 mentions, one of them simply "I refuse to enter this information twice, so bye" ([App Store RSS CA](https://itunes.apple.com/ca/rss/customerreviews/id=1038369065/sortby=mostrecent/json); [GB](https://itunes.apple.com/gb/rss/customerreviews/id=1038369065/sortby=mostrecent/json)). Smaller themes are unwanted AI chat and AI images, sexual-content banners on the home screen, notification settings that are ignored, canned support replies, and gaps in inclusivity: no same-sex partner option, nothing for users after a hysterectomy, temperature charts locked to Fahrenheit, and a birth-year list that excludes 13-year-olds.

Privacy is a small but intense theme, with about 15 reviews, all 1★. It is fed by Flo's legal history. The FTC settled with Flo in 2021 over sharing health data with Facebook, Google, AppsFlyer and Flurry. In the Frasco class action, **Google ($48M), Flo ($8M) and Flurry ($3.5M) settled for $59.5M**, and Meta went to trial and lost. A jury found in August 2025 that Meta violated California's privacy law, and Meta says it will appeal ([The Record](https://therecord.media/jury-verdict-meta-flo-app-data-privacy-case); [HIPAA Journal](https://www.hipaajournal.com/flo-health-google-flurry-59-5m-settlement-privacy-lawsuit/)). Reviewers often garble the facts ("they just lost a lawsuit for selling their users data to Meta"). The garbling matters less than the effect: the story works as a final reason to leave, and at least one reviewer moved to Stardust because of it ([App Store RSS US](https://itunes.apple.com/us/rss/customerreviews/id=1038369065/sortby=mostrecent/json)). Flo's 2022 Anonymous Mode helps, but it is opt-in and **cannot recover data if the device is lost** ([Campaign](https://campaignlive.com/article/period-tracker-app-flo-turns-anonymous-mode-will-ease-user-privacy-concerns/1799360)). That trade-off collides directly with the data-loss complaints.

### Neutral and mixed points: strong UX, but partner mode and content draw mixed reviews

The 533 positive reviews praise **accuracy for regular cycles (about 51 mentions), pregnancy and TTC support (about 91, the largest positive theme), clean UX and the widget**. One user switched from Clue because Flo was "so much EASIER to navigate" and did not harass them, which suggests upsell intensity varies between users ([App Store RSS CA](https://itunes.apple.com/ca/rss/customerreviews/id=1038369065/sortby=mostrecent/json)). Symptom logs work as a health record that makes "conversations with my healthcare providers so much easier." Several features are genuinely mixed. The content library gets about 74 positive and 67 negative mentions; critics call it generic ("The same articles for 9 months"). Perimenopause mode is "so useful" to one user and to another "a smattering of info … certainly not worth $35 every single month," with no way to log HRT. **Partner mode is valued in principle and called thin in practice**: "the partner access is extremely lacking. Almost no point to have it." The partner sees phases but not symptoms or mood, and same-sex couples cannot link ([App Store RSS AU](https://itunes.apple.com/au/rss/customerreviews/id=1038369065/sortby=mostrecent/json)). The typical 3★ verdict is "would be 5 stars without the same constant promotion ad."

### Improvements Flo users ask for, and features a competitor could add

Users explicitly ask for a free core tracker, or a cheaper or **one-off purchase**, and for pop-ups they can opt out of. They ask for **custom symptoms and moods**; condition flags for PCOS and endometriosis "so it doesn't keep … guessing why you have xyz symptoms when you already know"; contraception logging (implant, IUD); more postpartum symptoms and breastfeeding-aware predictions; IVF tracking and transfer dates; and pregnancy-loss handling. They want diagnostic alerts that can be dismissed, ovulation-test results that update predictions, Apple Health sync restored, and fuller use of Apple Watch and Garmin data. Other requests are partner sharing of symptoms and mood with same-sex linking, a permanent Celsius setting, time on chart axes, a more prominent cycle-day number, content toggles for sexual topics, a chatbot users can skip, human support, and an in-app feedback channel ([App Store RSS AU](https://itunes.apple.com/au/rss/customerreviews/id=1038369065/sortby=mostrecent/json); [CA](https://itunes.apple.com/ca/rss/customerreviews/id=1038369065/sortby=mostrecent/json); [Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=org.iggymedia.periodtracker&hl=en_US&gl=US)). Notably, **"export", "PDF" and "download my data" got zero hits in the sample**. Mainstream users do not ask for export, but they feel its absence when they lose data or try to switch.

From this, a competitor could build the following:

- An honest free core, with transparent one-time pricing if there is any price at all.
- A cycle-context profile that the prediction engine actually uses.
- Range-based predictions that explain themselves and can be dismissed.
- Smooth transitions from pregnancy to postpartum to cycling, and from pregnancy loss back to cycling.
- Two-way health integrations.
- Notification controls that are honoured.
- A richer, gender-neutral partner mode.
- Encrypted backup controlled by the user.
- One-tap import from Flo.

Users who switch already go to Clue, Stardust, Apple Health's Cycle Tracking, the free "Balance" app for perimenopause, or simply their calendar or notes app ("I'd rather use my calendar at this point because it's free") ([App Store RSS AU](https://itunes.apple.com/au/rss/customerreviews/id=1038369065/sortby=mostrecent/json)).

## Clue and Stardust trade the same paywall for different trust problems

Clue's complaints mirror Flo's at lower intensity. Cycle-length and symptom analysis (Recurrent Symptoms Analysis), parts of the Content tab, pregnancy symptoms, history views and reportedly export have **moved into Clue Plus**. Users also report "an add began popping up after every log" and no way to switch promotions off without paying ([App Store US](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/clue-period-cycle-tracker/id657189652); [Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.clue.android&hl=en_US); [LeafSnap](https://leafsnap.com/clue-period-tracking-fertility-insights-and-reproductive-health-support/)). Its partner feature, Clue Connect, requires the sharer to pay (about $39.99 a year), connects only one person, shares only calendar markers, and **does not work in pregnancy mode** ([thevibecheck.app](https://thevibecheck.app/blog/relationship-advice/clue-connect-partner-feature-free-paid)). Clue cannot import data from other apps and **cannot restore its own export** ([Clue support](https://support.helloclue.com/hc/en-us/articles/21540631889181-Can-I-import-my-data-to-the-Clue-app)). Privacy is the reason users stay. In July 2026, Mozilla scored Clue **8/10**, behind only Euki, and praised its separate, revocable consents for research, analytics and advertising ([Mozilla](https://www.mozillafoundation.org/en/nothing-personal/clue-period-tracker-app-privacy-review/); [Captain Compliance](https://captaincompliance.com/education/mozilla-tested-six-period-tracker-apps-only-one-earned-a-perfect-privacy-score/)). Accuracy is good for regular cycles and weak for irregular ones. A designer who worked on Clue's perimenopause mode found that as cycles become irregular, predictions lose usefulness; in her survey of 1,044 users of the mode, 53% were satisfied and 42% were neutral ([Marianne Yates](https://www.marianneyates.com/ux-ui/clue-perimenopause-mode---marianne-yates)).

Stardust has the opposite profile: **4.8★ on iOS and Mozilla's lowest privacy score, 2/10**. Mozilla found it was the only app of the six it tested sharing detailed reproductive-health data, including birth-control method and reproductive goals, with another company (RudderStack). Stardust says that data goes only to its own analytics ([Mozilla](https://www.mozillafoundation.org/en/nothing-personal/stardust-privacy-review/); [ppc.land](https://ppc.land/mozilla-rates-stardust-2-of-10-over-symptom-data-sent-to-third-parties/)). This follows its 2022 episode, when it promised end-to-end encryption after the Dobbs decision. TechCrunch then found phone numbers going to an analytics firm, and Stardust's policy said it would cooperate with law enforcement "whether or not legally required" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2022/06/27/stardust-period-tracker-phone-number/)). Users rate it on fun: astrology, moon phases, hormone-phase explanations and partner messaging ([Bustle](https://www.bustle.com/wellness/stardust-app-review-period-tracking-astrology)). Their complaints are about stability: blank screens, slow launches, crashes when a notification is tapped, data resets after updates and login loops. Users also report a paywalled feature that used to be free, billing after cancellation, a 28-day default for irregular cycles, and an age gate that blocks an 11-year-old ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.stardust.app&hl=en_US); [justuseapp](https://justuseapp.com/en/app/1495829322/stardust-period-tracker/reviews)).

## Fertility apps win on tools and lose on billing and data sharing

The four fertility-focused apps share a pattern: users praise **specific tools**, while **subscription billing and privacy history** generate most of the anger. Natural Cycles users report charges "month after month after cancellation," auto-renewal without notice, and cancellation that has to happen on the website ([Trustpilot](https://www.trustpilot.com/review/www.naturalcycles.com); [Healthline](https://www.healthline.com/health/natural-cycles-review)). Its other recurring frustration is an excess of Red Days (days the app marks as potentially fertile). Natural Cycles keeps a help article on why they happen: irregular cycles, missed measurements, recently stopping the pill, switching devices and perimenopause ([NC Help](https://help.naturalcycles.com/hc/en-us/articles/360003363813-Why-do-I-get-so-many-Red-Days)). Independent multi-country data put typical-use effectiveness at a **Pearl Index of about 5–7.5** ([Chief Healthcare Executive](https://chiefhealthcareexecutive.com/view/how-the-first-fdaapproved-contraception-app-performs-across-populations)). The 2018 Stockholm report, in which 37 of 668 abortion seekers had used the app, remains its defining controversy ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2018-01-15-natural-cycles-app-unwanted-pregnancies.html)). Users like wearable input from Oura and Apple Watch for its convenience, but the equivalence claims come from the company. Switching between a thermometer and a wearable resets the temperature baseline and brings more Red Days for about a cycle ([NC Help](https://help.naturalcycles.com/hc/en-us/articles/13558176506909-Can-I-switch-to-a-thermometer-after-using-a-wearable-device)).

Ovia's problem is structural. Employers who offer it as a benefit could see aggregated employee data, and in 2022 Mozilla still advised users to refuse employer sharing ([Boston Globe](https://www.bostonglobe.com/business/2019/04/10/menstrual-monitoring-app-raises-questions-about-privacy/bRdtxn094ZO9ImN0zlKBuN/story.html); [Mozilla](https://foundation.mozilla.org/privacynotincluded/ovia-fertility/)). Users like its pregnancy toolkit (daily articles, a kick counter, a contraction timer that one reviewer credits with getting her to hospital in time, and partner sharing) but complain about ads, **no Apple Health import and poor miscarriage handling** ([The Everymom](https://theeverymom.com/ovia-fertility-pregnancy-app-review/); [App Store reviews](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/ovia-cycle-pregnancy-tracker/id570244389?see-all=reviews&platform=undefined)). Glow and Eve users say "pretty much everything aside from the tracker and polls are premium," including **viewing their own health history**. They also report refused refunds on lifetime purchases and lost data when Eve was merged into Glow ([JustUseApp](https://justuseapp.com/en/app/1002275138/period-tracker-eve/reviews); [Glow Community](https://glowing.com/community/topic/6856027/glow-need-to-stop)). Glow's 2016 Partner Connect flaw let an account be linked without consent and led to a **$250,000 California AG settlement in 2020** ([Consumer Reports](https://www.consumerreports.org/mobile-security-software/glow-pregnancy-app-exposed-women-to-privacy-threats-a1100919965/); [WilmerHale](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20200929-california-settles-with-glow-app-over-alleged-privacy-and-security-violations)). Premom's ovulation-test photo reader, which turns LH strip lines into a numeric ratio and curve, is called its "killer feature," and users praise its BBT chart with a coverline. Recent updates, though, put **past cycles and historical charts behind a paywall**, scans are "not always accurate" for the first 2–3 cycles, and the FTC and three states settled with its maker for $200,000 in 2023 over sharing health data without consent ([FertileStart](https://fertilestart.com/blog/46-best-fertility-apps.html); [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/05/ovulation-tracking-app-premom-will-be-barred-sharing-health-data-advertising-under-proposed-ftc)).

## Calendar apps and wearables leave users without backups or their own data

Simple calendar trackers are popular because they are simple. Period Calendar holds **about 4.9★ from about 4.9M Play reviews** and is praised as "really slick and easy to use" with "generally pretty accurate" predictions ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.popularapp.periodcalendar&hl=en); [Grand Screen](https://grand-screen.com/apps/period-calendar-period-tracker/)). Their users complain about ads they "cannot exit out of," subscription fatigue ("when every app wants a subscription it adds up"), and ad-tech data flows. Mozilla called Period Calendar "the only ad-supported app we tested" and scored it 6/10, after its home screen sent device details to DoubleClick and AdMob ([Mozilla](https://www.mozillafoundation.org/en/nothing-personal/period-calendar-privacy-review/); [JustUseApp](https://justuseapp.com/en/app/896501514/perioden-kalender-zyklus-app/reviews); [Sensor Tower](https://app.sensortower.com/api/android/apps/com.lbrc.PeriodCalendar?country=US)). Their worst failure is **data loss on a new phone**: "I have not backed up anything so I lost all my info" ([App Store: GP Apps](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/period-tracker-by-gp-apps/id330376830)). Mozilla advises privacy-minded users to skip Period Calendar's Google or Apple cloud backup, which removes their only safe migration path. Averages built from stale data also fail: after three years on the pill, one user's average cycle came out at **138 days** ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.popularapp.periodcalendar&hl=en_NZ)).

Wearable owners are not short of data. They are short of **access to their data and context for it**. Apple's ovulation estimates are retrospective only. They need a Series 8 or later and about two cycles of overnight wear, and they can fail when the temperature shift is too small, so they arrive too late for conception timing ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/120357)). Apple Health exports a 12-month PDF for clinicians, but its full export is one unfiltered XML file ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/en-us/120356)). Its partner sharing is one-way and thin for symptoms and mood ([PeriodBro](https://periodbro.com/apple-health-cycle-tracking/)). After an update, Samsung Health showed temperature only as a deviation such as "+/- 0.2 C." Users say the update "retroactively changed my temperatures," readings show one decimal place, and they ask, "how about just share my own skin temperature readings with me?" ([Samsung Community](https://eu.community.samsung.com/t5/mobile-apps-services/cycle-tracking-temperature-display-in-samsung-health-update/td-p/12722916)). Fitbit and Pixel did not tie the temperature sensor to cycles, and a March 2025 update **erased skin-temperature history** that depends on a baseline built over several nights ([Android Central](https://www.androidcentral.com/wearables/does-google-pixel-watch-2-have-temperature-sensor); [Android Police](https://www.androidpolice.com/pixel-watch-march-update-issues/)). Garmin users call cycle tracking "barebones" and "way to separate from everything else." They ask for "a graph that ties temps to the menstrual cycle and not to sleep," and for **HRV and running pace compared across phases**. One reports historical tracking vanishing after an update ([Garmin Forums](https://forums.garmin.com/apps-software/mobile-apps-web/f/garmin-connect-mobile-andriod/412522/menstrual-cycle-tracking-underwhelming)). Oura claims its November 2025 update makes period predictions 21% more accurate for irregular cycles, but the validation comes from Oura-affiliated authors and Oura is not cleared for PCOS ([Oura blog](https://ouraring.com/blog/oura-cycle-insights-update/); [Talk to Rox](https://www.talktorox.com/blogs/oura-ring-for-pcos)). Bellabeat's dominant complaint is sync that "doesn't always work." Its cycle tracking is "rudimentary," with a 15–40 or 18–40 day cycle range depending on the source, and scores are paywalled after the hardware is bought ([Garage Gym Reviews](https://www.garagegymreviews.com/bellabeat-leaf-fitness-tracker-review); [Trustpilot](https://ie.trustpilot.com/review/www.bellabeat.com?page=2)).

## Privacy-first apps keep the data but miss the conveniences

The private and open-source apps win on trust. **Euki got Mozilla's only 10/10 in July 2026**, with no account, local storage and no health data leaving the device ([CyberInsider](https://cyberinsider.com/mozilla-study-ranks-euki-as-the-most-private-period-tracker/)). Even so, Mozilla noted that the app's name alone can reveal that someone uses a reproductive-health app, and that its PIN and scheduled data deletion have to be turned on by the user ([Mozilla](https://www.mozillafoundation.org/en/nothing-personal/euki-privacy-review/)). Their issue trackers show what users miss compared with Flo and Clue. drip does not show fertile days in the calendar; the team calls changing that "a huge rebuild project" ([drip FAQ](https://dripapp.org/faq.html)). drip also predicts only after three complete cycles, and users ask for dark mode and a friendlier interface ([AlternativeTo](https://www.alternativeto.net/software/drip-/about/)). Its oldest open requests are **automatic encrypted backups (#430, open since 2020)**, whose reporter argued drip cannot replace Clue without them, and scheduled exports to Drive or Nextcloud (#549). In 2026 users added requests to exclude atypical cycles from statistics (#809), to show an approximate fertile phase without temperature data (#804), to make the bleeding field optional for a user who will never bleed (#806), for a medication tracker with a paracetamol and ibuprofen daily-limit warning (#810), and for iCal export to share with a partner who will not install the app (#818). Accessibility bugs also appear: larger system fonts break the chart, and calendar dates overflow their cells ([drip GitLab](https://gitlab.com/bloodyhealth/drip/-/issues); [#430](https://gitlab.com/bloodyhealth/drip/-/issues/430); [#809](https://gitlab.com/bloodyhealth/drip/-/issues/809); [#806](https://gitlab.com/bloodyhealth/drip/-/issues/806); [#810](https://gitlab.com/bloodyhealth/drip/-/issues/810)). Migration is fragile in both directions. drip's FAQ points Flo users to an online converter, and a user objected that someone leaving Flo for privacy is told to upload their most personal data to a site with no privacy policy (#794). A Femm user could not work out drip's CSV format (#787), and community converters exist for Fitbit and Natural Cycles ([drip #794](https://gitlab.com/bloodyhealth/drip/-/issues/794); [drip #787](https://gitlab.com/bloodyhealth/drip/-/issues/787); [NaturalCyclesToDrip](https://github.com/AmeliaMN/NaturalCyclesToDrip)).

Periodical uses the least accurate prediction method, the calendar (Knaus-Ogino) method, requests no internet permission, and offers only manual backup to storage ([F-Droid](https://f-droid.org/en/packages/de.arnowelzel.android.periodical)). Mensinator's issue list shows that privacy-minded users expect protection that goes **beyond "no server"**. They asked for screenshots and the recent-apps preview to be blocked, the database to be encrypted at rest, Android autobackup to Google to be turned off, the keyboard to stop learning symptom words, and a discreet alternative icon. They also asked for a bottom navigation bar for one-handed use, a medication section, widgets and ovulation prediction ([Mensinator issues](https://github.com/EmmaTellblom/Mensinator/issues?q=is%3Aissue+sort%3Acomments-desc)). Read Your Body users miss calendar views and forecasts ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/read-your-body/id1488117813)). Partner-focused apps show the design range. Cycles keeps notes and reminders private even from the partner but requires Premium and an Apple device for the partner. DaysyDay lets the user choose which categories to share, and Flo for Partners reportedly shows education rather than data ([Woman & Home](https://womanandhome.com/health-wellbeing/best-period-tracking-apps); [DaysyDay](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/daysyday-cycle-period-tracker/id1533071047); [PeriodBro](https://periodbro.com/flo-for-partners-review/)).

## Nine unmet needs recur across every category

Taken together, the evidence shows nine needs that no single app meets.

The **first is an honest, permanent free core**. Paywall creep appears in Flo, Clue, Glow, Premom, Stardust, Bellabeat and Period Calendar, and users read paywalled history as their own data held hostage.

The **second is predictions that understand context**: conditions, contraception, coming off the pill, postpartum, perimenopause and pregnancy loss. Users also want predictions shown as ranges with explanations, alerts they can dismiss, and no labelling of their bodies as "late." A 2026 qualitative study quotes a PCOS user for whom constant "late" labels "became a self-fulfilling prophecy" ([arXiv 2605.13261](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.13261)).

The **third is data permanence**. History disappears through phone changes (GP Apps, drip), vendor updates (Garmin, Pixel), product merges (Eve), unexplained resets (Flo, Stardust), and privacy modes that cannot recover data (Flo Anonymous Mode).

The **fourth is portability**. Clue cannot import or restore its own export, export formats do not match between apps, and users write their own converters ([Clue support](https://support.helloclue.com/hc/en-us/articles/21540631889181-Can-I-import-my-data-to-the-Clue-app); [iTechGuides](https://www.itechguides.com/how-to-export-and-back-up-period-tracking-data-without-cloud-sync/)).

The **fifth is verifiable privacy** rather than slogans. Flo, Stardust, Premom, Glow, Ovia and Period Calendar all carry privacy history, and the private apps show that users want screenshot blocking, encryption at rest and discreet naming.

The **sixth is wearable data with context**: absolute temperatures, combined prospective and retrospective ovulation estimates, and HRV and sleep by phase.

The **seventh is partner sharing that is granular, free for both people and cross-platform**. It should work during pregnancy, keep notes private, and suit any couple.

The **eighth is continuity across life stages**, from TTC to pregnancy, then postpartum and breastfeeding, then perimenopause. Clue retired its postpartum product ([Clue](https://helloclue.com/articles/about-clue/important-news-about-clue-postpartum)). Natural Cycles is one of the few that switches to postpartum-bleeding tracking with no predictions until ovulation or a period ([NC Help](https://help.naturalcycles.com/hc/en-us/articles/360003291094-How-to-use-the-app-while-breastfeeding)).

The **ninth is depth for specific conditions, and reports a clinician can read**. Endometriosis patients mainly use Flo and Clue but most want pain, bowel and mental-health tracking and are willing to share with clinicians ([Scheck et al. 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/22840265231193486)). Doctor-ready PDFs are mostly sold as premium or found only in niche apps.

Two smaller but consistent threads run alongside these. One is inclusion: same-sex partners, users who never bleed, users after a hysterectomy, and teens ([UW News](https://www.washington.edu/news/?p=53022)). The other is accessibility, meaning font scaling, contrast and one-handed navigation.

These needs are well evidenced in kind but poorly quantified outside Flo. Reddit communities (r/PCOS, r/endometriosis, r/Perimenopause, r/TryingForABaby) were unreachable, so the relative ranking of condition-specific needs rests on open-source issues, vendor positioning and one survey.

## Conclusion

The market's central failure is a business model: monetising the user's own history and attention. Tracking technology is not the problem. That reframes competition. An app with no paywall, no ads and local-first storage, which describes Avelune, has already removed the cause of most one-star reviews in this sample, and it no longer needs to win on those terms. The remaining fights are about **understanding the user's body over a lifetime** and **guaranteeing that their record outlives any phone, vendor update or company decision**. Those are exactly the areas where incumbents create switching moments: a postpartum user told she has PCOS, a Garmin owner who cannot see her own temperatures, a ten-year Flo user whose history vanishes.

A second, less obvious implication is that portability works as acquisition, not just as a feature. Flo users do not ask for export, yet they talk about their years of history as the reason they stay, and privacy migrants are being sent to untrusted upload sites to move that history. An app that imports Flo, Clue and Apple Health data entirely on the device turns the incumbents' lock-in into an on-ramp. Finally, private apps show that privacy alone does not keep users. drip's longest-standing open requests are automatic backups and a fertile window shown in the calendar. The winning position combines Euki-grade privacy with Flo-grade convenience, and the evidence shows no current app occupies it.
